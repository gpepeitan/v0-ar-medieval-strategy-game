# MedievalAR Partner Pitch

## One-line pitch

MedievalAR is a persistent real-world medieval strategy game where players claim public land, build supply networks, manage economies, command armies, and fight over territory in a world that never resets.

## The big idea

Most strategy games are either abstract board games or pure combat games. MedievalAR is different: it treats geography, logistics, and long-term power as the actual game.

The real world is the map. Public-access land becomes the strategic surface. Roads, bridges, chokepoints, plazas, routes, and terrain matter. Players do not just raise armies — they build living power structures that depend on food, labor, transport, defense, and command.

The result is a game about medieval statecraft, but grounded in the real world and designed for modern persistent multiplayer.

## Why this game is compelling

### 1. It has a strong fantasy

The player fantasy is simple and powerful:
- build a domain
- defend it
- expand it
- control routes and chokepoints
- outmaneuver rivals
- create something that lasts

That fantasy is bigger than just combat. It is about becoming a serious power in a living world.

### 2. It has real strategic depth

The game is built around systems that interact:
- territory and claims
- movement and route control
- production and labor
- supply and stockpiles
- trade and tolls
- armies and commanders
- morale and siege pressure
- faction identity
- counterplay and anti-exploit rules

A strong army is not enough if it is undersupplied. A wealthy city is not enough if its routes are cut. A fortress is not enough if it has no labor, repairs, or relief.

### 3. It supports many viable playstyles

Players can win through:
- farming and food control
- mining and industry
- trade and route taxation
- mobile raiding
- defensive fortification
- chokepoint control
- siege warfare
- mixed-arms field battles
- logistics and supply warfare

The goal is not to make one perfect build. The goal is to make the world support many real paths to power.

### 4. It creates long-term attachment

Because the world is persistent, what players build matters.
A castle is not just a building. A trade route is not just a line. A commander is not just a stat block.
Players grow attached to territory, structures, dynasties, and reputations because those things persist and can be lost or defended over time.

## What makes MedievalAR different

### Persistent world, never reset

The world does not wipe. Power changes hands, but the game never restarts.

### Public-access land only

The game is intentionally constrained to public-access geography. That makes it more playable, safer, and more strategic.

### Structures upgrade individually

There is no global settlement tier. A location grows through specific buildings and infrastructure, which creates real choice and specialization.

### Logistics matter more than raw numbers

You cannot just spam units and win forever. Supply, route control, morale, and command quality all matter.

### Military secrecy is partial, not total

The world map and claims are visible, but military movement and exact force details require scouting or local observation. That keeps the game readable without killing strategy.

## Core gameplay loop

1. Scout public land and route opportunities.
2. Claim useful territory.
3. Build farms, markets, workshops, outposts, forts, castles, and route infrastructure.
4. Produce food, wood, stone, iron, and other strategic goods.
5. Train commanders and raise armies.
6. Move caravans and forces along routes.
7. Tax, trade, defend, raid, negotiate, and siege.
8. Recover from losses and grow the dynasty.

The game is not just about winning fights. It is about building something worth defending.

## The economy

The economy is local first and trade second.

Resources have different values, rarity, and strategic importance. Food is always important. Stone and wood matter because they build and repair. Iron matters because it turns production into power. Horses are rare and highly strategic. Gold is valuable, but not required for basic survival.

This creates a real economy where players have to choose:
- do I grow food?
- do I fortify?
- do I invest in trade?
- do I build military capacity?
- do I specialize in scarce resources?

A healthy economy should not be a single optimal loop. It should have multiple strong shapes with different weaknesses.

## The military

Armies are not just damage numbers.
They are combined systems of:
- infantry
- cavalry
- ranged support
- siege units
- logistics and support units

Different unit types counter different threats. Mixed armies are usually better than spammed armies, but no composition should be universally best.

Morale matters. Supply matters. Terrain matters. Commanders matter.

This makes war feel like war instead of a raw numbers contest.

## The social and political layer

The game also supports:
- route politics
- tolls and safe passage
- diplomacy and trade agreements
- faction identity
- long-term reputation
- dynasty continuity

That gives players a reason to interact beyond combat. Not every rivalry has to be solved with war.

## Why this can attract players

The game has several hooks that can attract different audiences:

- strategy players want depth and planning
- MMO players want persistence and territory
- history players want grounded medieval systems
- geography players want real-world map strategy
- competitive players want counterplay and territory conflict
- social players want alliances, trade, and political power

It can become a game where players care about their local region, their route network, their holdings, and their reputation.
